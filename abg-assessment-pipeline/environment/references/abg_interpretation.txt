BLOOD GAS INTERPRETATION — Systematic Approach
=================================================

Use the following steps to approach blood gas interpretation in a comprehensive
and structured manner.

STEP 1. pH
----------
A normal pH is between 7.35 and 7.45 (use 7.4 as the normal value for all
calculations).

  If pH < 7.35 → acidaemia
  If pH > 7.45 → alkalaemia

If pH is normal, there could be no acid-base disorder, or there could be
multiple concurrent processes (e.g. a metabolic acidosis + metabolic alkalosis
cancelling out).

STEP 2. Assess PaCO2 and HCO3 to Identify the Primary Disorder
----------------------------------------------------------------
Normal PaCO2: 35–45 mmHg (use 40 mmHg as normal)
Normal HCO3: 22–26 mmol/L (use 24 mmol/L as normal)

If acidaemia:
  Reduced HCO3 indicates metabolic acidosis
  Elevated PaCO2 (with HCO3 not reduced) indicates respiratory acidosis

If alkalaemia:
  Elevated HCO3 indicates metabolic alkalosis
  Reduced PaCO2 (with HCO3 not elevated) indicates respiratory alkalosis

STEP 3. Consider the Cause of the Primary Disorder
----------------------------------------------------
For metabolic acidosis: Calculate the anion gap to differentiate HAGMA vs NAGMA.
For metabolic alkalosis: Consider chloride-responsive vs chloride-unresponsive.

STEP 4. Assess for Secondary Processes
---------------------------------------
Calculate expected compensation and compare to measured values. If compensation
is not appropriate, an additional concurrent disorder is present.

See compensation_formulas.txt for the specific equations and tolerances.

In the context of a high AG metabolic acidosis, compute the delta ratio to
assess whether there is a single process or a mixed disorder. See
delta_ratio.txt for interpretation thresholds.

STEP 5. Apply Corrections to Measured Values
---------------------------------------------
Correct for albumin effects on anion gap (see anion_gap.txt).
Correct sodium for hyperglycaemia.
Correct potassium for pH.
Assess the osmolar gap (see osmolar_gap.txt).
Consider the physicochemical (Stewart) approach (see stewart_approach.txt).
