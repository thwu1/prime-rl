ROTATING MACHINERY BALANCE ANALYSIS - REFERENCE NOTES

1. STATIC AND DYNAMIC BALANCE OF ROTATING BODIES

A rotating body is in STATIC BALANCE when there is no resultant centrifugal
force, i.e. the centre of gravity lies on the axis of rotation.

For a mass M at radius R rotating at angular velocity omega, the centrifugal
force is F = M * omega^2 * R. Since omega^2 is common to all masses, balance
depends solely on the mass-radius products (MR) and their angular positions.

A rotating body is in DYNAMIC BALANCE when, in addition to static balance,
there is no resultant turning moment along the axis. For bodies with masses
distributed along the shaft, dynamic balance requires consideration of the
moments of the centrifugal forces about a reference plane.

For complete balance of a general system:
  (a) The vector sum of all mass-radius products must be zero.
  (b) The vector sum of all mass-radius-distance products must be zero,
      where distance is measured from a reference plane.

Two correction planes are sufficient to balance any number of masses in any
number of planes. By choosing one correction plane as the moment reference,
its unknown contribution to the moment equation vanishes, allowing the other
correction to be found from the moment balance alone. The first correction
then follows from the force balance.

Each MR product is a vector quantity with magnitude M*R directed at the
angular position of the mass. Moment vectors are formed by multiplying
MR vectors by their axial distance from the reference plane.


2. RECIPROCATING ENGINE BALANCE

For an inline piston-crank-connecting-rod mechanism, the acceleration of
the piston has two harmonic components:

  PRIMARY: varies with crank angle theta at shaft speed
  SECONDARY: varies with twice the crank angle (2*theta)

The ratio n = connecting_rod_length / crank_radius governs the relative
magnitude of the secondary component.

For a single cylinder:
  Primary inertia force peak = M * omega^2 * R
  Secondary inertia force peak = M * omega^2 * R / n

For multiple inline cylinders, each cylinder's contribution can be
represented as a rotating vector (like a rotating mass) at the appropriate
angle. Primary vectors use the crank angle; secondary vectors use the
doubled crank angle.

Force balance: the vector sum of all per-cylinder contributions must vanish.
Moment balance: the vector sum weighted by axial distance from a reference
plane must vanish.

When force or moment balance is not achieved, the residual vector magnitude
(times omega^2) gives the peak unbalanced force or moment.

Primary balance corrections can be applied using the same two-plane
reference-plane method as rotating mass balance.


3. FLYWHEEL ENERGY ANALYSIS

A flywheel stores kinetic energy: E = (1/2) * I * omega^2, where I is the
moment of inertia (I = M * k^2, with k the radius of gyration).

Machines with cyclic torque variation (engines, compressors, presses) cause
the shaft speed to fluctuate. A flywheel limits this fluctuation by acting
as an energy buffer.

The coefficient of fluctuation of speed:
  Cs = (omega_max - omega_min) / omega_mean

The mean torque equals the total work per cycle divided by the cycle angle.
The energy fluctuation above and below the mean determines the maximum
energy variation during one cycle.

The maximum energy fluctuation delta_E is found by computing the cumulative
integral of (T - T_mean) over the cycle and taking the difference between
its maximum and minimum values.

The relationship between delta_E and flywheel sizing:
  delta_E = I * omega_mean^2 * Cs

This allows determination of the required moment of inertia for a desired
coefficient of fluctuation, and from I = M * k^2, the required flywheel mass.

The maximum and minimum shaft speeds follow from:
  omega_max = omega_mean * (1 + Cs/2)
  omega_min = omega_mean * (1 - Cs/2)
