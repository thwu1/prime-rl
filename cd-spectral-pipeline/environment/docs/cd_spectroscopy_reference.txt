CIRCULAR DICHROISM SPECTROSCOPY: CONVENTIONS AND ANALYSIS METHODS
================================================================


1. MEASUREMENT AND UNITS

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy measures the differential absorption of left-
and right-circularly polarized light by chiral molecules. CD instruments typically
report signal in millidegrees of ellipticity (theta, mdeg), which reflects the raw
instrument output.

For meaningful comparison between proteins of different sizes and concentrations,
the raw ellipticity must be normalized. Two standard normalized units are in common
use:

Mean Residue Ellipticity (MRE, [theta]):
  The raw signal in millidegrees is normalized by the mean residue weight (MRW),
  the optical pathlength, and the sample concentration. MRW is defined as the
  protein molecular weight divided by the number of amino acid residues. The
  normalization accounts for the approximately equal contribution of each peptide
  bond to the far-UV CD signal. The standard relationship is:

  [theta] = theta_obs * MRW / (10 * c * l)

  where theta_obs is in millidegrees, c is the protein concentration in mg/mL,
  l is the cuvette pathlength in cm, and MRW = MW / n_residues. The resulting
  units of [theta] are deg*cm^2*dmol^-1.

Delta Epsilon (delta_eps):
  The molar circular dichroism per residue, expressed in M^-1*cm^-1. The
  relationship between mean residue ellipticity and delta epsilon is:

  [theta] = 3298 * delta_eps

  This conversion factor (3298) derives from the relationship between ellipticity
  and differential absorbance: theta(radians) = ln(10) * delta_A / 4, evaluated
  per mean residue and converted to degrees.

All three representations (mdeg, [theta]/MRE, delta_eps/de) are interconvertible
when the sample parameters (concentration, molecular weight, residue count, and
optical pathlength) are known. Note that the conversion between millidegrees and
either normalized unit requires all four parameters, whereas converting between
MRE and delta epsilon requires only the factor of 3298.


2. SECONDARY STRUCTURE ESTIMATION BY BASIS-SPECTRUM DECONVOLUTION

The far-UV CD spectrum of a protein (approximately 190-260 nm) is dominated by
contributions from the polypeptide backbone. Different secondary structural elements
produce characteristic spectral signatures:

  - alpha-helix: strong negative bands near 208 nm and 222 nm, positive band near
    192 nm
  - beta-strand: weaker negative band near 218 nm, positive band near 196 nm
  - turns: variable features in the 190-210 nm region
  - unordered (coil): strong negative band near 198 nm

The observed protein spectrum can be modeled as a linear combination of pure
secondary structure basis spectra:

  S_obs(lambda) = f_H * S_helix(lambda) + f_S * S_strand(lambda)
                + f_T * S_turn(lambda) + f_C * S_coil(lambda)

where f_i are the fractional contributions of each structural class. Because these
fractions represent physical proportions of the protein's secondary structure content,
they are subject to two fundamental constraints:

  (i)  Each fraction must be non-negative: f_i >= 0 for all i
  (ii) The fractions must sum to unity: sum(f_i) = 1.0

Analysis programs such as DichroWeb, CDPro, and SELCON3 enforce these constraints
during the optimization procedure. In practice, the fitting is performed as a
constrained least-squares minimization, finding the fraction vector that minimizes
the sum of squared residuals between the observed and reconstructed spectra subject
to the above constraints.

The quality of the deconvolution fit is assessed using the Normalized Root Mean
Square Deviation (NRMSD):

  NRMSD = sqrt( sum( (S_obs - S_calc)^2 ) / sum( S_obs^2 ) )

where the sums are taken over all wavelength points. An NRMSD below 0.10 generally
indicates an acceptable fit quality. Values above 0.25 suggest the basis set does
not adequately represent the observed spectrum.

When the observed spectrum and basis spectra are defined on different wavelength
grids, linear interpolation onto a common grid is necessary before performing the
deconvolution.


3. THERMAL DENATURATION ANALYSIS

CD signal at a characteristic wavelength (e.g. 222 nm for helical proteins) can be
used to monitor thermal unfolding. In the simplest and most widely used model, the
protein unfolds cooperatively between a folded native state (N) and an unfolded
state (U):

  N <=> U

The temperature-dependent equilibrium constant K relates to thermodynamic parameters
through the van't Hoff equation:

  K(T) = exp[ dH_vH / R * (1/T_m - 1/T) ]

where T_m is the midpoint temperature at which K = 1 (the protein is 50% unfolded),
dH_vH is the van't Hoff enthalpy of unfolding in kJ/mol, and R is the gas constant
(8.314 x 10^-3 kJ*mol^-1*K^-1). Temperature T is in Kelvin.

The observed spectroscopic signal at temperature T is a population-weighted average
of the native and unfolded signals:

  theta(T) = [ theta_N(T) + theta_U(T) * K(T) ] / [ 1 + K(T) ]

Importantly, both the native-state and unfolded-state signals exhibit intrinsic
temperature dependence (baseline drift). This is modeled with linear functions:

  theta_N(T) = a_N + b_N * T
  theta_U(T) = a_U + b_U * T

The complete model therefore has six adjustable parameters: a_N, b_N (native
baseline intercept and slope), a_U, b_U (unfolded baseline intercept and slope),
T_m (melting temperature), and dH_vH (van't Hoff enthalpy). These are determined
by nonlinear least-squares regression against the experimental theta-vs-T data.

The fraction of protein remaining in the native (folded) state at each temperature
is:

  f_folded(T) = 1 / (1 + K(T))

This decreases from approximately 1.0 at temperatures well below T_m to
approximately 0.0 at temperatures well above T_m.


4. SPECTRAL COMPARISON AND SIMILARITY METRICS

Quantitative comparison of CD spectra is essential for database searching and
spectral matching applications such as DichroMatch. The standard distance metric
is the Normalized Root Mean Square Deviation, defined as:

  NRMSD(A, B) = sqrt( sum( (S_A - S_B)^2 ) / sum( S_A^2 ) )

where A denotes the query spectrum and B denotes the reference spectrum. The
summations are taken over all wavelength points in the common grid.

When comparing spectra recorded on different wavelength grids, both must first be
interpolated onto a common grid spanning their mutual wavelength overlap before
computing the distance. Lower NRMSD values indicate greater spectral similarity.
